# Chapter V: Surfaces

**Source:** Hartshorne, V.1-V.6, printed pp. 356-423. A surface here is nonsingular and projective over an algebraically closed field. Restore characteristic restrictions when consulting the classification discussion.

## Core idea

Use the intersection pairing and the canonical class to measure curves on a surface, then track these classes through blowups and contractions. Surface classification requires controlling birational models as well as numerical invariants.

## Methods

### Intersection numbers as a bilinear calculation

The pairing on divisor classes is symmetric, bilinear, and invariant under linear equivalence. For effective curves meeting properly, local intersection multiplicities count the contribution at each point. On a nonsingular surface, curves with local equations `f,g` and isolated intersection at `P` have local multiplicity `length O_{X,P}/(f,g)`.

**Use when:** calculating degree, self-intersection, genus, or positivity.

1. Choose generators for the relevant divisor or numerical classes.
2. State their intersection matrix.
3. Express the divisors in that basis and calculate bilinearly.
4. Preserve the distinction between equality of divisors, linear equivalence, and numerical equivalence.
5. Interpret the resulting number using the theorem whose assumptions are met.

A self-intersection is the degree of a normal bundle or an intersection-class calculation, not the cardinality of a curve intersected with itself. Negative self-intersection is possible even for an effective irreducible curve.

### Adjunction and Riemann-Roch

For a nonsingular curve `C` on `X`, V.1.5 gives

\[
2g(C)-2=C\cdot(C+K_X).
\]

This calculates the curve's intrinsic genus from ambient divisor classes. For singular curves, distinguish arithmetic genus from the genus of the normalization before using an analogous formula.

Surface Riemann-Roch (V.1.6) is

\[
\chi(O_X(D))=\chi(O_X)+\tfrac12D\cdot(D-K_X).
\]

Here `chi(O_X)=1+p_a=1-q+p_g`, with `q=h^1(O_X)` and `p_g=h^0(omega_X)`. Duality identifies `h^2(O_X(D))` with `h^0(O_X(K_X-D))`. The middle group `H^1` generally remains: numerical intersection data compute the Euler characteristic, not automatically the dimension of a linear system.

**Why it works:** adjunction translates the canonical bundle of the curve into restrictions from the surface; Riemann-Roch translates an alternating cohomology count into intersection data. **Failure mode:** treating the Euler characteristic as `h^0` without a vanishing argument.

### Positivity and the Hodge index theorem

If `H` is ample and `D` is numerically nonzero with `D.H=0`, then `D^2<0` (V.1.9). The numerical qualifier matters: a nontrivial divisor can be numerically zero. Use this to constrain possible intersection matrices and test proposed numerical relations.

The Nakai-Moishezon criterion on a surface (V.1.10) says that `D` is ample exactly when `D^2>0` and `D.C>0` for every irreducible curve `C`. Checking only a handful of curves is enough only if an additional argument shows they control all curve classes. Positive self-intersection alone does not establish ampleness.

### Ruled surfaces

For a geometrically ruled surface `pi:X=P(E) -> C`, choose a section `C_0` coming from a normalized rank-two bundle and let `f` be the class of a fibre. With Hartshorne's invariant `e=-deg E`, the numerical relations are

\[
C_0^2=-e,\qquad C_0.f=1,\qquad f^2=0,
\]

\[
K_X\equiv-2C_0+(2g(C)-2-e)f.
\]

The canonical formula is a numerical equivalence; a divisor-class formula retains the relevant line bundle from the base. Also `Pic(X) ~= Z[C_0] direct-sum pi^*Pic(C)`, whereas the numerical group is generated by `C_0,f`. Do not collapse the entire Picard group of a positive-genus base to its degree.

For the Hirzebruch surface `F_e=P(O_{P^1} direct-sum O_{P^1}(-e))`, take `e>=0`. A divisor `aC_0+bf` is ample exactly when `a>0` and `b>ae`. Keep this convenient criterion attached to `F_e`; normalized bundles over other curves require their own analysis. An arbitrary ruled surface's invariant need not be nonnegative.

### Blowups and strict transforms

Let `pi:X' -> X` be the blowup at a nonsingular point, with exceptional curve `E`. V.3 gives

\[
\operatorname{Pic}(X')\simeq\pi^*\operatorname{Pic}(X)\oplus\mathbb Z[E],
\]

\[
(\pi^*D).(\pi^*D')=D.D',\quad (\pi^*D).E=0,\quad E^2=-1,
\]

\[
K_{X'}=\pi^*K_X+E,\qquad K_{X'}^2=K_X^2-1.
\]

If an effective curve `C` has multiplicity `m` at the center, its total transform and strict transform satisfy `pi^*C=C'+mE`. Consequently `(C')^2=C^2-m^2` and `C'.E=m`. Subtract the exceptional multiplicity before calculating the strict transform's intersections.

To resolve a local plane-curve singularity, calculate in the affine blowup charts, divide out the exceptional factor to obtain the strict transform, locate remaining singularities, and repeat as needed. Merely pulling back the equation gives the total transform.

### Contractions and minimal models

Castelnuovo's contraction theorem (V.5.7) applies to an irreducible curve `E ~= P^1` with `E^2=-1` on a nonsingular projective surface. It contracts `E` to a nonsingular point on another nonsingular projective surface. A negative curve with a different self-intersection does not satisfy this theorem.

Birational maps of nonsingular projective surfaces factor through point blowups and their inverses (V.5.5). Relative minimality means there is no further birational contraction to a nonsingular surface. Unlike curves, surface models within a birational class are not unique. V.5.8.4 gives uniqueness of the minimal model outside the rational and ruled cases; preserve those exceptions.

### Classification by pluricanonical behavior

Record the plurigenera `P_m=h^0(O_X(mK_X))` and the growth of the canonical ring. Hartshorne uses `kappa=-1` when every positive plurigenus is zero; modern notation often writes `-infinity` for this case. His remaining values for surfaces are `0,1,2`.

| Value in Hartshorne | Direction for study |
|---|---|
| `-1` | Rational and ruled birational classes |
| `0` | K3, Enriques, abelian, and hyperelliptic cases in the stated characteristic range |
| `1` | Elliptic fibrations in the stated characteristic range |
| `2` | General type and pluricanonical maps |

This is a routing table, not a classification proof. Read V.6 with the minimal-model context and its restrictions, particularly `char k!=2,3` in the indicated statements. A formula involving `K_X^2` or the linear class of `K_X` must specify the model: blowing up changes `K_X^2`, although plurigenera and Kodaira dimension are birational invariants. The book's research-status comments are historical.

## Worked example: blow up the plane and recover the cubic surface's lines

Reconstruct the calculations in V.3-V.4. First blow up a point of `P^2`, and let `H` denote the pullback of a line. Then

\[
H^2=1,\quad H.E=0,\quad E^2=-1,\quad K=-3H+E.
\]

The strict transform of a line through the center has class `F=H-E`. Therefore `F^2=0`, `F.E=1`, and

\[
F.(F+K)=(H-E).(-2H)=-2.
\]

Adjunction gives genus zero. The class `F` supplies the ruling on `F_1`; the exceptional curve is a section of self-intersection `-1`. A line avoiding the center has class `H`, not `H-E`.

Now blow up six distinct points with no three collinear and not all six on a conic, as in the general-position setup of V.4. Write `E_1,...,E_6` for the exceptional curves. Then

\[
-K=3H-\sum_{i=1}^6E_i,\qquad (-K)^2=9-6=3.
\]

The anticanonical embedding gives a nonsingular cubic surface. The line classes are:

| Origin of the curve | Class | Number |
|---|---|---:|
| Exceptional curve | `E_i` | 6 |
| Line through two centers | `H-E_i-E_j` | 15 |
| Conic through five centers | `2H-sum_{j!=i} E_j` | 6 |

Each class has self-intersection `-1` and intersection one with `-K`, so each maps to a line under the anticanonical embedding. For example, `(2H-sum_5 E_j)^2=4-5=-1` and `(-K).(2H-sum_5 E_j)=6-5=1`. The existence and completeness of this list use the general-position arguments and V.4.9, not just these numerical equalities. The total is `6+15+6=27`.

## Key concepts

- **Numerical equivalence:** divisors have the same intersection number with every curve.
- **Irregularity:** `q=h^1(O_X)`.
- **Geometric genus:** `p_g=h^0(omega_X)`.
- **Arithmetic genus:** `p_a=chi(O_X)-1=p_g-q` for a surface.
- **Exceptional curve:** the inverse-image curve of the blown-up point, with self-intersection `-1`.
- **Strict transform:** closure of the inverse image away from the blowup center.
- **Geometrically ruled surface:** a projective-line bundle over a nonsingular projective curve.
- **Plurigenus:** the dimension `h^0(O_X(mK_X))`.

## Anti-patterns and takeaways

1. Total transforms and strict transforms have different divisor classes.
2. Negative self-intersection does not by itself authorize a smooth contraction.
3. Riemann-Roch leaves an `H^1` term; intersection numbers alone need not determine a linear system.
4. A numerical equality is weaker than a linear equivalence.
5. State the model before quoting its canonical self-intersection.
6. Keep rational and ruled exceptions when claiming uniqueness of a minimal model.
7. Use characteristic restrictions from the actual classification theorem.
