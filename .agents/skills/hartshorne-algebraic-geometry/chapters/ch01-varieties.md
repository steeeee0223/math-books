# Chapter I: Varieties

**Source:** Hartshorne, I.1-I.8, printed pp. 1-59. Work over a fixed algebraically closed field `k`. In this chapter, a variety is irreducible; reducible zero sets are algebraic sets.

## Core idea

Move between polynomial equations and geometric objects through coordinate rings, then use localization and function fields to separate local geometry from birational geometry. Keep examples available to test whether a proposed global statement follows from its algebraic analogue.

## Methods

### The affine algebra-geometry dictionary

**Use when:** a geometric claim can be expressed by equations on an affine variety.

1. Set `A(Y)=k[x_1,...,x_n]/I(Y)`.
2. Translate inclusion of closed sets into reverse inclusion of radical ideals.
3. Translate irreducibility into primeness, hence into the coordinate ring being a domain.
4. For a point corresponding to a maximal ideal `m`, pass to `A(Y)_m`; for a nonempty irreducible affine variety, pass to `Frac(A(Y))` for rational functions.
5. Translate a `k`-algebra map back into a morphism in the opposite direction.

The key input is the Nullstellensatz: `I(Z(a))=sqrt(a)`. It is not an equality with `a` in general. The algebraically closed field is part of this statement: real point sets, for example, do not recover ideals in the same way. A coordinate-ring computation establishes a statement about the chosen affine objects, not automatically about arbitrary projective varieties.

**Why it works:** regular functions provide a concrete contravariant description of affine varieties. **Failure mode:** taking radicals too early destroys the multiplicities that later scheme theory needs.

### Projective geometry by homogeneous data and affine charts

**Use when:** equations, maps, or dimension computations are projective.

1. Check homogeneity and invariance under simultaneous nonzero rescaling of coordinates.
2. Cover by `U_i={x_i!=0}` and use the ratios `x_j/x_i` as affine coordinates.
3. Prove the local statement in each needed chart and check compatibility.
4. For homogeneous polynomials proposed as coordinates of a map, locate their common zero set; the formula is not a morphism there.

For a projective variety, its homogeneous coordinate ring depends on the embedding. It is not its ring of global regular functions. In particular, a projective variety has only constant global regular functions, despite having nonconstant rational functions. The Segre and Veronese constructions are useful explicit models; they carry different embedding data even when the underlying variety is familiar.

### Rational maps and function fields

**Use when:** comparing varieties on dense open sets or attempting a parametrization.

1. Specify a nonempty open domain where the proposed map is regular.
2. For a dominant rational map `X -->> Y`, construct the induced injection `K(Y) -> K(X)`.
3. To prove birationality, show this field map is an isomorphism, or construct inverse maps on dense open sets.
4. If the conclusion is an isomorphism of whole varieties, separately check extension across omitted points and regularity of the inverse there.

For an irreducible variety, nonempty open subsets are dense. This makes rational-function comparison effective, but it also explains why a rational inverse can miss exceptional loci. Birational invariants cannot classify all embeddings or singular models.

### Singularity testing

**Use when:** deciding whether a point of an affine variety `Y` is nonsingular.

- If `Y` has dimension `r` in `A^n` and its ideal is generated by `f_1,...,f_s`, evaluate the Jacobian at the point. Nonsingularity means rank `n-r`.
- Intrinsically, the local ring is regular when `dim_k(m/m^2)=dim O_{Y,P}` at a closed point. The tangent space is the dual of `m/m^2`.
- If the question is about formal local shape, complete the local ring. An isomorphism of completions is an analytic isomorphism in this chapter's terminology; it is a weaker conclusion than an isomorphism of local rings or of Zariski neighborhoods.

Use generators of the actual defining ideal, not arbitrary set-theoretic equations with extra powers. In positive characteristic, check whether differentiation has erased essential terms. Chapter I's algebraically closed hypothesis matters to the Jacobian discussion.

### Curves via valuations

**Use when:** compactifying a curve or comparing complete nonsingular curve models.

For a one-dimensional noetherian local domain, being a DVR, being regular, and being integrally closed are equivalent (I.6.2A). Valuation rings supply the missing points of a function field. The resulting nonsingular projective model is unique up to isomorphism. A rational map from a nonsingular curve to a projective variety extends across its missing points (I.6); do not export this extension principle to arbitrary higher-dimensional sources or nonproper targets.

### Dimension and degree

**Use when:** studying projective intersections or checking an embedding calculation.

For subvarieties of dimensions `r,s` in `P^n`, each component of a nonempty intersection has dimension at least `r+s-n`; if `r+s>=n`, the intersection is nonempty. This is a lower bound, not a guarantee of the expected dimension.

For a projective variety of dimension `r`, the Hilbert polynomial has degree `r`; its leading coefficient is `deg(Y)/r!`. Degree records intersections with sufficiently general linear spaces, counting multiplicity. A count of distinct intersection points can be smaller. For two plane curves of degrees `d,e` with no common component, Bezout's count is `de` with intersection multiplicities; the no-common-component condition is essential.

## Key concepts

- **Algebraic set:** a polynomial zero locus, possibly reducible.
- **Affine variety:** an irreducible closed subset of affine space.
- **Quasi-affine variety:** an open subset of an affine variety.
- **Projective variety:** an irreducible homogeneous zero locus in projective space.
- **Regular function:** a function locally expressible as a polynomial quotient with nonvanishing denominator, using suitable affine charts.
- **Rational map:** an equivalence class of morphisms on nonempty open subsets.
- **Function field:** the field of rational functions of an irreducible variety.
- **Normalization:** replacement by a normal variety with the same function field, obtained affinely by integral closure.
- **Regular local ring:** a noetherian local ring whose maximal ideal has as many minimal generators as its dimension.
- **Hilbert polynomial:** the polynomial agreeing with the graded coordinate-ring dimension in sufficiently large degrees.

## Worked example: a bijective birational map that is not an isomorphism

Reconstruct the cuspidal cubic example used around I.3-I.4. Let

\[
C=V(y^2-x^3)\subset\mathbb A^2,
\qquad \nu:\mathbb A^1\longrightarrow C,\quad t\longmapsto(t^2,t^3).
\]

Its coordinate-ring map identifies `A(C)` with `k[t^2,t^3]` inside `k[t]`. Both have function field `k(t)`, because `t=t^3/t^2` in the fraction field. Hence `nu` is birational.

For a point of `C` with `x!=0`, the only possible parameter is `t=y/x`, and the equation verifies `t^2=x` and `t^3=y`. The only point with `x=0` is the origin, whose preimage is `t=0`. Thus the map is bijective on the classical points.

Nevertheless, `t` is not in `k[t^2,t^3]`. It is not even regular at the cusp. If `t=a/b` in the localization at `(t^2,t^3)`, then `b` has nonzero constant term, so `tb` has a nonzero term of degree one. No element `a` of `k[t^2,t^3]` has such a term. Thus no regular inverse exists near the cusp.

The local ring of the cusp has dimension one and two independent generators of `m/m^2`, namely the classes of `x,y`, so it is singular. The element `t` is integral over `A(C)` since it satisfies `T^2-x=0`; adjoining it gives the normalization `k[t]`.

**What to retain:** point-set bijectivity, birationality, normality, and isomorphism answer different questions. Inspect the induced ring map and the local ring at the exceptional point.

|---|---|---|
| Irreducibility | Defining radical ideal is prime | A connected zero set |
| Birationality | Isomorphic function fields | Equal dimension or degree |
| Isomorphism of affine varieties | Isomorphic coordinate rings | A bijection on points |
| Nonsingularity at a point | Regular local ring or correct Jacobian rank | A picture without self-crossing |
| Equality of embedded schemes | Equality of defining ideal sheaves | Equality of supports |

## Anti-patterns

- **Use the product topology for a product variety.** The Zariski topology on the geometric product is generally finer than the product of the Zariski topologies; the diagonal on `A^1 x A^1` detects the difference.
- **Assume every open subset of an affine variety is affine.** Principal opens are affine, but arbitrary opens require an argument.
- **Identify a homogeneous coordinate ring with global functions.** The former remembers the embedding and grading.
- **Confuse normal and nonsingular in every dimension.** Their equivalence for curves does not persist for higher-dimensional varieties.
- **Treat a Hilbert polynomial as the Hilbert function in every degree.** Equality is eventual.

## Mental models and takeaways

1. Choose the algebraic object matching the geometric scale: coordinate ring, local ring, completion, or function field.
2. A rational parametrization controls a dense open set; its missing points need separate analysis.
3. Use singular and nonnormal examples to test an argument that relies only on closed points.
4. Separate intrinsic invariants from invariants of an embedding.
5. Classification starts by choosing an equivalence relation; birational classification and isomorphism classification are different tasks.
