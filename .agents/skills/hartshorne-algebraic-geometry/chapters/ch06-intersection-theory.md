# Appendix A: Intersection Theory

**Source:** Hartshorne, Appendix A.1-A.5, printed pp. 424-437. `ch06` is this skill's file identifier. The appendix is a survey of higher-dimensional methods, not a construction of every theorem from first principles.

## Core idea

Replace numerical intersections of divisors on a surface by products of cycle classes, then use Chern classes to convert vector-bundle information into this intersection language. Riemann-Roch extracts an Euler characteristic from the top-degree part of a characteristic-class expression.

## Methods

### Cycles and rational equivalence

A codimension-`r` cycle is an integral linear combination of integral closed subvarieties of that codimension. A subscheme contributes multiplicities given by the lengths of its local rings at the relevant generic points. Passing to its support first discards these multiplicities.

Rational equivalence is generated by principal-divisor relations on subvarieties, using normalization in Hartshorne's formulation. Let `A^r(X)` be codimension-`r` cycles modulo rational equivalence. For nonsingular quasi-projective varieties, these groups form the Chow ring under intersection.

**Use when:** a question concerns intersecting subvarieties, degrees of cycles, or characteristic classes.

1. Fix the codimension grading and the class of varieties where the intersection product is available.
2. Replace cycles by rationally equivalent representatives meeting properly, using the moving lemma.
3. Compute local multiplicities at the generic points of the intersection components.
4. Reassemble the cycle class; take its degree only when it is a zero-cycle on a proper variety.

Do not interchange rational, algebraic, numerical, and homological equivalence without a theorem. They quotient by different relations.

### Intersection multiplicity and higher Tor

For properly intersecting subvarieties `Y,Z` of a nonsingular variety, and a component `W` of their intersection, let `A=O_{X,W}` and let `a,b` be their ideals in this regular local ring. The appendix describes Serre's formula

\[
i(Y,Z;W)=\sum_{j\ge0}(-1)^j
\operatorname{length}_A\operatorname{Tor}^A_j(A/a,A/b).
\]

The regular ambient local ring and proper-intersection hypotheses control finiteness. The tensor-product length is only the `j=0` term; higher terms can change it.

**Why it works:** the alternating Tor length records the failure of the two structures to meet without homological interference. **Failure mode:** carrying the surface formula `length A/(a+b)` into higher dimensions without checking the higher Tor groups.

### Functoriality and projection

For a proper map, a cycle whose image drops dimension pushes forward to zero; otherwise multiply the image cycle by the degree of the function-field extension. Properness is required for the induced operation on rational-equivalence classes in the stated framework.

Use the projection formula

\[
f_*(\alpha\cdot f^*\beta)=f_*\alpha\cdot\beta
\]

to move a computation to a space where the classes are simpler. In this appendix, pullback for morphisms between smooth varieties is an intersection-theoretic operation defined through the graph. It is not an unrestricted pullback of arbitrary cycles by set-theoretic inverse image. Reduction to the diagonal interprets `Y.Z` as `Delta^*(Y x Z)`.

### Chern classes and the splitting principle

For a locally free sheaf `E` of rank `r`, its Chern classes `c_i(E)` lie in `A^i(X)`. The projective-bundle relation uses Hartshorne's quotient convention and `xi=c_1(O_{P(E)}(1))`:

\[
\xi^r-\pi^*c_1(E)\xi^{r-1}+\cdots+(-1)^r\pi^*c_r(E)=0.
\]

Use these calculation rules:

- `c(O(D))=1+D`.
- Pullback commutes with Chern classes.
- For an exact sequence of locally free sheaves, `c(E)=c(E')c(E'')`.
- After a suitable pullback injective on Chow groups, use a filtration with line-bundle quotients. If their first Chern classes are formal roots `a_i`, compute with `c(E)=product(1+a_i)` and rewrite the result symmetrically.
- A section of a rank-`r` bundle whose zero scheme has codimension `r` represents `c_r(E)`. Retain the codimension condition.
- For a nonsingular closed subvariety, self-intersection is controlled by the top Chern class of its normal bundle.

The splitting principle does not assert that the original vector bundle is a direct sum of line bundles on `X`.

### Hirzebruch-Riemann-Roch

For a locally free sheaf `E` on a nonsingular projective variety `X` of dimension `n`, A.4.1 gives

\[
\chi(E)=\deg\bigl(\operatorname{ch}(E)\operatorname{td}(T_X)\bigr)_n.
\]

The subscript means the codimension-`n` component. Work in the Chow ring with rational coefficients and truncate expansions above dimension `n`. Useful low-degree formulas are

\[
\operatorname{ch}(E)=r+c_1(E)+\tfrac12(c_1(E)^2-2c_2(E))+\cdots,
\]

\[
\operatorname{td}(T_X)=1+\tfrac12c_1(T_X)
+\tfrac1{12}(c_1(T_X)^2+c_2(T_X))+\cdots.
\]

For `O(D)`, `ch(O(D))=exp(D)`. Remember `c_1(T_X)=-K_X`, not `K_X`. Vanishing theorems are a separate step if the desired answer is `h^0` rather than `chi`.

A.5.3 presents Grothendieck-Riemann-Roch for a **smooth projective morphism** `f:X -> Y` of nonsingular quasi-projective varieties:

\[
\operatorname{ch}(f_!x)=f_*(\operatorname{ch}(x)\operatorname{td}(T_f)),
\qquad f_![F]=\sum_i(-1)^i[R^if_*F].
\]

Use the alternating higher direct image, not just `f_*F`. The appendix mentions further generalizations; do not silently replace its displayed theorem with a broader statement.

## Worked example: recovering surface Riemann-Roch

Reconstruct A.4.1.2. Let `X` be a nonsingular projective surface and `D` a divisor. Set `K=K_X` and `c_2=c_2(T_X)`.

1. In dimensions at most two,

   \[
   \operatorname{ch}(O(D))=1+D+\tfrac12D^2,
   \quad \operatorname{td}(T_X)=1-\tfrac12K+\tfrac1{12}(K^2+c_2).
   \]

2. Only products whose codimensions sum to two contribute:

   \[
   \chi(O(D))=\tfrac12D^2-\tfrac12D.K
   +\tfrac1{12}\deg(K^2+c_2).
   \]

3. Set `D=0` to identify the last term as `chi(O_X)`. Therefore

   \[
   \chi(O(D))=\chi(O_X)+\tfrac12D.(D-K).
   \]

For `X=P^2`, let `H` be a line, so `K=-3H`, `H^2=1`, and `c_2(T_{P^2})=3H^2` from the Euler sequence. Substitution gives `chi(O(d))=(d+1)(d+2)/2`, agreeing with projective-space cohomology. For `d>=0`, higher cohomology vanishes and this is also `h^0`; the Euler-characteristic formula itself holds for every integer `d`.

### Diagnostic example: tensor-product length overcounts

Reconstruct A.1.1.1. In `A^4`, take the union of the planes `(x,y)` and `(z,w)`, whose ideal is `(xz,xw,yz,yw)`. Intersect it with the plane `(x-z,y-w)`. Each component meets this plane transversely at the origin, so bilinearity gives intersection multiplicity two.

The ordinary quotient instead is `k[x,y]/(x^2,xy,y^2)`, with basis `1,x,y`, hence length three. The higher Tor terms correct the discrepancy. This is a concrete reason to retain the homological formula.

## Key concepts and decision checks

| Concept | Working role |
|---|---|
| Chow group | Cycles modulo rational equivalence |
| Proper intersection | Every intersection component has the expected codimension |
| Moving lemma | Replaces representatives without changing their rational-equivalence classes |
| Chern root | Formal line-bundle class used in symmetric-polynomial calculations |
| Chern character | Additive characteristic class, multiplicative for tensor products |
| Todd class | Correction factor in Riemann-Roch |
| Degree | Numerical extraction from a zero-cycle on a proper variety |

## Takeaways

1. State the grading and equivalence relation before calculating with cycles.
2. Use higher Tor terms unless their vanishing is justified.
3. In Riemann-Roch, use tangent-bundle classes and keep only top degree.
4. The splitting principle is a calculation after pullback, not a splitting assertion on the original space.
5. An Euler characteristic becomes a section count only with suitable vanishing.
6. The Nakai-Moishezon generalization tests positive intersection on all positive-dimensional integral subvarieties, not only curves in dimension greater than two.
